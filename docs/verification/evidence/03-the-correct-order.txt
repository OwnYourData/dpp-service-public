  The previous record measured this move with the two acts in the wrong order
  and found the gap that produces. This one measures the same move in the order
  that follows from the finding: custody first, DNS second, release last.


1 — Baseline, made unambiguous first

  B still held the copy it had served before the earlier move, so "both answer"
  would have been true without anything being moved. B was therefore emptied
  first, under an admin-scoped token at the custodian itself:

      DELETE https://dpp.data-vault.eu/collection/4/purge   {"count":2,"events":5}
      Store.where(key: "object_4").count                     0

  This is also the release act the third step calls for, performed in advance:
  the service exposes release only as a parameter of the move
  (release_previous=true), which is right for a zone with a short time-to-live
  and wrong for this one. Retiring a custodian after the fact is the operator's
  own act at their custodian, carried out under their own mandate.

  State: A serves, B does not, the name points at A.


2 — Act one: custody

      POST /dpp/v1/dpps/{dppId}/custody
      X-DPP-Storage: base_url https://dpp.data-vault.eu, collection_id 4,
                     a delegation minted for B

      {"digitalProductPassportId":"did:oyd:zQmdX5gYVMPyfbTDmy4cv7guhchbHQBiTu64vyVvZMNxMdf",
       "uniqueProductIdentifier":"https://dpp.oydapp.eu/01/09520123456791/21/000123",
       "dppStatus":"Active"}

  Neither identifier changes. That is the whole claim: the string that was
  printed on the product is not a party to this transaction.

  Measured with both custodian addresses pinned by curl --resolve, so that no
  cache can decide the outcome:

      carrier at 89.58.20.114 (A)   200
      carrier at 152.53.34.232 (B)  200
      long form at dpp.go-data.at        200
      long form at dpp.data-vault.eu     200

  Both addresses answer. This is the overlap, and it is the state in which act
  two cannot fail.

  On where this was measured, because it decides whether the two lines above
  mean anything: from the operator's own machine, not from the observation host
  used elsewhere in this run. That host sits behind an egress proxy which
  resolves names itself and silently ignores curl --resolve -- pinning a name to
  203.0.113.99, an address reserved for documentation where nothing is listening,
  still returned 200 there. Every address attribution in this record therefore
  comes from a vantage point where a counter-check against that same unreachable
  address fails as it should. The check is cheap and belongs in any measurement
  that claims to have asked a particular host.


3 — Act two: DNS, under observation

  A sampler polled both pinned addresses and the name itself every 3 seconds
  across the change of the A record from A to B.

      18:09:23Z ... 18:11:42Z    41 samples
      A=200  B=200  viaDNS=200   in every single one

  No deviation. Compare the previous record, where the same two acts in the
  opposite order produced a 404 whose only reason for being a single one was
  that the sampler's cache happened to hide the others.


4 — The measurement that shows why the overlap is not optional

  At 18:12:29Z, one minute after the change, two independent public resolvers
  were asked the same question and gave different answers:

      dns.google           dpp.oydapp.eu   A   152.53.34.232   TTL 820
      cloudflare-dns.com   dpp.oydapp.eu   A   89.58.20.114    TTL 7200

  Both are correct from where they stand: one has the new record, the other is
  still serving a valid cached answer of the old one. There is no moment at
  which the world agrees on where the name points -- there is only a period in
  which both answers are in circulation, bounded by the zone's time-to-live,
  here 7200 s.

  Both answers worked, because both custodians were serving. That is what the
  overlap buys, and it is why releasing the previous custodian belongs after the
  time-to-live rather than with the move.


5 — Three statements, and why they need not agree

  A custodian can be named in three places, and they move independently:

      the DNS record in the operator's zone      moved in act two
      the DID document's serviceEndpoint         not moved
      the service's own custody record           moved in act one

  Resolution of the passport DID after both acts:

      id               did:oyd:zQmdX5gYVMPyfbTDmy4cv7guhchbHQBiTu64vyVvZMNxMdf
      canonicalId      null
      serviceEndpoint  https://dpp.data-vault.eu/dpp/v1/dppsByProductId/
                       https%3A%2F%2Fdpp.oydapp.eu%2F01%2F09520123456791%2F21%2F000123

  canonicalId is null: this DID document has never been updated. It has named B
  since the passport was created. During the period the passport was held at A,
  it therefore sent a reader to B -- and the reader still received the passport,
  because B was still serving.

  Stated plainly rather than glossed: the DID document was stale for that
  period, and only the overlap made the staleness harmless. Bringing the
  serviceEndpoint along is a separate, explicit act, and in did:oyd it costs a
  new identifier, because the identifier is the hash over the document (see the
  serviceEndpoint record). An operator who wants the DID document to stay
  accurate pays that price per move; an operator who does not, relies on the
  overlap -- and the overlap has a deadline.


What this establishes

  A change of custodian costs a delegation, a log entry and a DNS record. It
  costs nothing that was printed, nothing that was registered, and no identifier
  anywhere. The one thing it does cost is time: the previous custodian has to
  keep serving for one time-to-live of the operator's zone, and the order of the
  two acts is not a matter of taste.
