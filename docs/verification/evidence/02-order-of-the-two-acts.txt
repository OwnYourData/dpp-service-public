  Moving a passport from one custodian to another is two acts that are not the
  same statement: the carrier moves by DNS, custody moves by a signed log entry
  at the service. This record is about the ORDER of the two, and it is written
  the way it happened rather than the way it was planned: the order was given
  wrong, the wrong order was measured, and the measurement is the point.


1 — What was done, in the order it was done

      17:03:44Z   sampler starts. Name resolves to B, carrier 200.
      17:05:05Z   first sample in which the local resolver returns A.
                  The DNS record had been changed to A a moment earlier.
      17:05:27Z   HTTP 404.
      <=17:06:16Z custody move B -> A completes; A now serves the passport.
      17:19:39Z   sampler stops. 173 samples, exactly one of them 404,
                  no sample without an answer, no gap above 15 s.

  The instruction that produced this said "change the DNS record" and, in the
  same paragraph, "the passport is at A before the name points there". Those two
  contradict each other. The DNS record was changed first, and the window
  between the two acts was therefore real: about 71 seconds in which the name
  pointed at a custodian that did not yet hold the passport.


2 — Why exactly one sample failed, and why that number is too small

  Of the 13 samples inside the window, 12 answered 200 and one answered 404.
  That ratio is not a failure probability, and this record does not claim to
  know which custodian answered any individual sample.

      authoritative answer, checked at 17:10Z through two independent public
      resolvers (dns.google, cloudflare-dns.com):

          dpp.oydapp.eu.   A   89.58.20.114   TTL 7200

  One record, pointing at A. But the observation host used for this sampler sits
  behind an egress proxy that resolves names itself: the dns= column of the log
  is what the host's own resolver returned, while the request went wherever the
  proxy sent it. The two need not agree, and the log cannot attribute an answer
  to an address. Stated plainly because the earlier draft of this record claimed
  otherwise.

  What the log does establish is that a gap existed at all: at 17:05:27Z a
  request over the printed carrier was refused, in the window between the name
  being repointed and the passport arriving. One refusal is enough to establish
  the gap and says nothing about its size. A client whose cache already held the
  new address would have been refused on every request in those 71 seconds.


3 — The finding this run actually produced

  The old address remained in circulation long after the record was changed: the
  sampler's own resolver was still handing out the previous answer minutes
  later, and the record's lifetime allows it for two hours. The same was visible
  from outside at 18:12Z, a minute after a later change, when two independent
  public resolvers gave two different answers for the same name (record 3).

  This is the quantity the overlap has to cover. The previous custodian cannot
  be released when the DNS record changes; it has to keep serving for at least
  one time-to-live of the operator's zone -- here 7200 s -- because until then
  readers keep arriving at the old address holding a perfectly valid cached
  answer. Leaving the previous custodian in service is therefore not a
  convenience of the implementation (POST /dpp/v1/dpps/{dppId}/custody releases
  the previous custodian only when release_previous=true is asked for); it is
  what the naming system requires.

  The correct order follows from the same fact, in reverse:

      1. move custody, without releasing the previous custodian
         -> both custodians serve; no cached answer can be wrong
      2. change the DNS record
         -> readers arrive at either address, both answer
      3. after one time-to-live, release the previous custodian

  Between steps 1 and 2 there is no window at all, because the failure mode of
  step 2 is a reader arriving at the NEW address, and after step 1 the new
  address answers. The wrong order inverts exactly this.


4 — What is measured next

  Record 3 measures the same move in the opposite direction, A -> B, in the
  order above. It is sampled from the operator's own machine rather than from
  here, because this observation host is an ephemeral container that is
  suspended between working steps -- the sampler above covers a continuous
  window, but it cannot be relied on to span a step taken elsewhere.

  The measurement also no longer waits for DNS to converge -- with a
  7200-second lifetime that would take hours and would say nothing the zone file
  does not already say. Instead both addresses are polled directly, pinned with
  curl --resolve, so the claim under test is the one that matters to a reader:

      at every moment of the transition, BOTH addresses answer 200,
      so no cache state can produce a failure.

  That is the property the wrong order destroys, and it is the property the
  paper asserts when it says a change of custodian costs a delegation, a log
  entry and a DNS record -- and nothing that was printed.
