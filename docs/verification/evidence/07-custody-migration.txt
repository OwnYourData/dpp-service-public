  POST /dpp/v1/dpps/{dpp_id}/custody with a delegation whose aud is
  https://dpp.data-vault.eu. The delegation issued for A does not hold there;
  the move is therefore an act of its own, signed by the holder.

2 — The certificate, before the switch

  Custodian B could not obtain a certificate of its own for dpp.oydapp.eu: the
  ACME HTTP-01 challenge goes wherever the name resolves, and that was still
  custodian A. A does not serve B's IngressClass, does not find the challenge,
  and Let's Encrypt issues nothing. Chicken and egg.

  Solved by transferring the existing certificate: the name belongs to the
  economic operator, so the certificate is his material. SAN: dpp.go-data.at,
  dpp.oydapp.eu, valid until 2026-11-20.

  Probe BEFORE the switch, against B's address, while the world still saw A:
      curl --resolve dpp.oydapp.eu:443:152.53.34.232 https://dpp.oydapp.eu/01/...
      -> 200, ssl_verify_result 0

  Continuity was thereby established before it was needed.


3 — The moment of the switch, measured from outside both clusters

  Five-second sampling of the unchanged data carrier:

      2026-08-23T12:29:05Z  dns=89.58.20.114    http=200  tls=0  0.560 s
      2026-08-23T12:29:11Z  dns=152.53.34.232   http=200  tls=0  0.559 s

  In the record, the change of custodian is a single step between two samples.
  No failure, no certificate error, no change in response time. Lead-in: 131
  samples over A, all 200.

  What the change cost: one delegation naming B (signed), one write into B's
  store, one A record in the economic operator's zone. No reprint, no new
  registration, no changed identifier in any downstream system.


4 — The closing proof: A cannot have answered

  The hostname was removed from custodian A's ingress. Afterwards, from the
  operator's machine, with a current resolver (over mobile data rather than the
  provider's resolver):

      curl --resolve dpp.oydapp.eu:443:89.58.20.114 https://dpp.oydapp.eu/01/...
          -> 404      custodian A no longer serves the name

      curl https://dpp.oydapp.eu/01/09520123456791/21/000123
          -> 200      the unchanged printed string returns the passport

  That is the core of it. The second answer cannot have come from A, because A
  answers the same name at the same address with 404. It came from custodian B,
  under the same string of characters that is printed on the data carrier.

  Together with section 3: the same printed string, answered by A before and by
  B afterwards, without a failure at the transition, and with the evidence that
  the old custodian could no longer answer.


5 — What retiring the old custodian costs

  During the same two minutes, measurements were taken from a third location
  whose resolver still held the old record: 22 samples, 16 of them 404. A
  request straight to B's address returned 200 with a valid certificate at the
  same moment -- so the custodian was working, the resolution was not.

  The authoritative nameservers (ns5/ns6.kasserver.com) both returned the new
  address at that point, as did 1.1.1.1 and 8.8.8.8. Individual resolvers held
  the old record regardless: the A record's time-to-live is 7200 seconds and is
  not configurable at the operator's DNS provider.

  The hostname was therefore restored at A; the final retirement follows once
  the TTL has expired and needs no further measurement.

  Conclusion: the change of custodian is instantaneous and lossless, because
  both can serve at the same time. Retiring the old one is a separate step whose
  cost is set not by the architecture but by the TTL in the economic operator's
  zone. The overlap is therefore not a convenience but a structural necessity --
  and the reason the custody operation does not release the previous custodian
  by default.


A note on the measurement

  The first observation window contains one failed sample at 12:07:36Z
  (http=000). It comes from the observer process blocking in name resolution in
  that second and then standing still until it was restarted. Three control
  requests immediately afterwards returned 200. It is an artefact of the
  measurement, not an outage of the measured system. The loop was subsequently
  given timeouts for resolution and fetch.

  In retirement.txt, resolution and fetch are separate operations: the address
  noted on a line and the address curl actually used can diverge. The status
  codes in that record are therefore meaningful as a proportion of failed
  requests, not as an attribution to a particular address. Attribution is done
  in section 4, where the address is pinned with --resolve.
